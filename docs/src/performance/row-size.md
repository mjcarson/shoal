# Row size

Generated by `shoal-bench render` from the artifacts committed under `docs/perf/`. **Do not edit this page by hand** - re-run the command instead, and `shoal-bench render --check` will tell you when it is out of date.

An even mixture of reads and writes, swept from a 64 byte row to a 4 MiB one, on every table. The three named mixtures at the end of the axis are declared distributions of widths rather than ranges, so two captures of one measure the same thing.

Rendered against commit `d40adf5` with uncommitted changes, on `jove` with the `performance` governor. [What has been captured](overview.md#what-has-been-captured).

**What this measures.** The same even mixture as the grid, swept across eleven row widths: eight fixed widths from 64 bytes to 4 MiB, and three declared mixtures of widths. The mixtures are named distributions rather than ranges - `mixed_small` is always the same four widths in the same proportion - so two captures of one measure the same thing.

**How to read it.** The x axis is logarithmic, so the *shape* is what to read rather than the magnitude. A cost that is flat across the narrow widths and rises past a kilobyte is a fixed per-response cost giving way to a per-byte one; a straight rising line is a per-byte cost the whole way. Throughput in **rows per second** and throughput in **bytes per second** tell opposite-looking stories at the two ends of this axis, and both are drawn, because a store that moves few large rows quickly is not slow.

**What would make it wrong.** Sample count at the wide end. Every arm moves roughly the same number of *bytes*, so the 4 MiB arm runs a few hundred queries where the 64 byte arm runs twenty thousand. **Read the p50 at the wide end**: a p99 over two hundred samples is roughly its third-worst observation and is not a percentile in any useful sense. The wide arms also seed a small key space - sixty four partitions at 4 MiB - so every read there is answered from a table that is entirely resident.

**What it cannot tell you.** What a wide row costs under a write-heavy mixture. This axis is swept at the reference mixture only, so an interaction between width and mixture would be invisible to both sweeps. The cross was chosen over the full cube deliberately; the gap is recorded in [todos](../appendix/todos.md).

On the unsorted table a read of a 64 B row took 36.56 µs and a read of a 4 MiB one took 26.86 ms: **65,536× the bytes for 734.6× the time.** The cost starts tracking the width somewhere above 512 KiB; below that a row is too narrow for its own size to be what the query is paying for.

## The mixed widths

Three arms draw each row's width from a declared set rather than using one width throughout. They are not on the charts above, and deliberately: a mixture's width is the **mean** of its distribution, and a mean plotted on the same axis as a measurement invites the two to be read the same way.

What they are for is the comparison in the table below. A mixture whose cost matches the fixed-width arm at its own mean is a system whose cost is linear across that range. A mixture that costs more than its mean says the widths in it are not interchangeable - that the wide rows in the distribution cost more than the narrow ones save.
